## Finding 3 (non-blocking): the sweep cannot see HTML-entity punctuation

`normalize()` strips line comments, collapses `{" "}` and folds whitespace,
but does not decode HTML entities. JSX prose in this repository is written
with entities — 709 occurrences of `&rsquo;`/`&ldquo;`/`&mdash;` across 26
files under `app/`. A tripwire whose phrase contains punctuation is therefore
blind to the house spelling of that phrase.

Probed in a sandbox copy (nothing written to the working tree). A new
`app/entity-page/page.js` containing:

    <p>The loop works inside rules it can&rsquo;t change.</p>

passes the check, exit 0. The identical sentence with a literal `’` is caught
(exit 1). So the tripwire works; it is the entity form it cannot see.

Today this touches one tripwire of eleven — `/can(?:’|')t change/gi`, the
lowest-value one — because the other ten are plain ASCII. It matters as a
property rather than as a live hole: every future tripwire containing an
apostrophe or a quote inherits it, and a dead regex is indistinguishable from
"the phrase is absent". Two tripwires currently match nothing (`may not
merge`, `can't change`), which is their correct state — they exist to catch
re-introduction — but nothing would tell you if one were dead by typo instead.

In fairness this is inherited, not introduced: `check-frame.mjs` reports its
attested count and does not assert it either. Same gap, one level up.

## Finding 5 (non-blocking): the allowance table is where the cost lives

This is the proportionality answer, and it is a qualified yes.

I instrumented the checker's `cover()` to record which list silenced each
sweep hit. Of the 26 hits: **19 are covered by an allowance** (prose only, no
predicate) and **7 fall inside a registered claim** (predicate-backed). So
73% of the sweep's output is bookkeeping whose only function is to keep the
check quiet.

Most of those 19 are the site's own corrections quoting the false text they
correct — which rule 5 *requires* a correction to do ("Corrections are new
entries that name what they correct"). The allowance table therefore grows
monotonically with every correction the site publishes: the check's
maintenance load scales with the activity it exists to support. That is the
design's real inefficiency, and it is the shorter thing that would have done
it — silence quoted-past-claims structurally (a correction wrapper, or a
`governance-correction:` marker the sweep skips) rather than one needle at a
time, and the 18-entry table shrinks toward zero.

Cost, measured: 794 lines in the check (146 comment, 48 blank, ~272 of the
remainder being registry *data* rather than logic — the engine and predicates
are roughly 290 lines), 210 in the test, 94 in `human-owned-paths.js`.
Runtime 92 ms for the check, 4.4 s for the test (it copies `app/` and
`.github/` into seven sandboxes).

Where it earns its keep, and I think it does:

- Generation, not the registry, is the best part of this round.
  `human-owned-paths.js` (45 lines of code) and the derived charter count
  make two of the six defects structurally impossible, not merely detected.
- The tripwire sweep alone would not have sufficed. Tripwires are
  retrospective — every phrase on the list is one that has already been
  wrong — so they catch *recurrence*, not first occurrence. Three of the six
  defects went false because the tree moved under stable prose, and no
  tripwire fires for that. Only the registry's predicate half addresses the
  actual 2026-08-22 failure mode, and it is not replaceable by anything
  shorter.
- Deletion was not available for most of it. The homepage hero and the
  `/blog` post are *about* the project's governance; deleting the claims
  would leave the site silent on its own central fact.
- The 4 `attested` entries check nothing and are documentation with a print
  statement. That is honest and I would keep them, but they are cost, not
  coverage.

On the registry going stale: it cannot, silently. A reworded page is a red
build naming the file and the truncated needle. The residual risk is
different — a future round updating a needle mechanically without re-reading
the predicate under it, turning the entry into a rubber stamp. Nothing in the
design can notice that.

## Finding 6 (non-blocking): the harness defect is described but unowned

Item 7 correctly diagnoses `check-routes.sh` reporting "127 check(s) failed"
with no FAIL line, and fixes the proximate cause (`process.exitCode` instead
of `process.exit()`, with the Windows `UV_HANDLE_CLOSING` abort explained in

idiom, which adds the *exit code* rather than 1 — and that idiom is at **31
call sites** in `check-routes.sh`, two of them added by this round. Anything
exiting with a large code still produces a nonsense count with no FAIL line.

It is described in the changelog and nowhere else: no docket item, and
`build`'s queue had room (9 -> 12 against a budget of 14). Pre-existing and
not this round's to fix, but "recorded in prose only" is how it recurs.

Minor, mentioned once: `test-governance-claims.mjs:196` reports
`${CASES.length - failures}/${CASES.length}` where `failures` can include a
control failure that is not one of `CASES`, so a broken control would report
5/6 rather than naming itself. Only misleads in a failure path.
