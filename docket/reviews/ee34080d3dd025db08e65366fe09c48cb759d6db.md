## 1. The statute — verified against the codified text, not a summary

Read in full from
`leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displayText.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&division=8.&title=&part=&chapter=25.&article=`
(HTTP 200, 153147 bytes), and cross-checked against the AB 853 bill text
(HTTP 200).

Verbatim confirmations — every one of these is exactly as the item states it:

- **§22757.6** — "This chapter shall become operative on August 2, 2026."
- **§22757.2(a)** — "A covered provider shall make available an AI detection
  tool at no cost to the user that meets all of the following criteria:"
- **Criterion (1)** — "The tool allows a user to assess whether image, video,
  or audio content, or content that is any combination thereof, was created or
  altered by the covered provider's GenAI system."
- **Six criteria**, and the item's one-line summary of (2)–(6) is accurate.
- **§22757.1(d)** — "'Covered provider' means a person that creates, codes, or
  otherwise produces a generative artificial intelligence system that has over
  1,000,000 monthly visitors or users and is publicly accessible within the
  geographic boundaries of the state." The item's quotation is exact.
- **$5,000 per violation**, each day a discrete violation (§22757.4(a)(1),
  (b)); **no private right of action** — correct.
- **AB 853 moved the date.** Confirmed two ways: §22757.6's amendment note
  ("Amended by Stats. 2025, Ch. 674, Sec. 6. (AB 853)") and the AB 853 text
  itself. Large online platforms 1 Jan 2027 (§22757.3.1(c)), capture device
  manufacturers 1 Jan 2028 (§22757.3.3(c)) — both correct.

### BLOCKING 1 — "Enforcement is the Attorney General's" is wrong

§22757.4(a)(1), as amended by AB 853 and as codified today:

> A violator of this chapter shall be liable for a civil penalty in the amount
> of five thousand dollars ($5,000) per violation to be collected in a civil
> action filed by **the Attorney General, a city attorney, or a county
> counsel**.

I confirmed this twice — in the codified chapter and in the AB 853 bill text
(`billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260AB853`, Section 5).

The item's own secondary source does not support "AG-only" either. Morgan
Lewis says: "enforcement authority is vested in **the attorney general and
other state actors**". So the item's Evidence line crediting Morgan Lewis with
"AG-only enforcement" attributes to that firm a reading it did not publish.

This matters beyond pedantry: city attorneys and county counsel are a
materially different enforcement surface from a single statewide office, and a
post about a law now in force that gets its enforcers wrong has gotten the law
wrong.

### BLOCKING 2 — caveat 1 misstates the covered-provider threshold

Caveat 1 reads: "The **>1,000,000-monthly-users-in-California** threshold is
not something this round could measure from any primary source."

The statute does not say that. The 1,000,000 figure carries no geographic
qualifier; the geographic clause attaches to *public accessibility*:

> ...a generative artificial intelligence system that has over 1,000,000
> monthly visitors or users **and** is publicly accessible within the
> geographic boundaries of the state.

Morgan Lewis reads it the same way: "GenAI services that have more than one
million monthly visitors or users **and are publicly accessible in
California**."

The item quotes the section correctly in "Why now" and again in "Done when" —
but wrongly in the caveat, and the caveat is the sentence that tells the author
round to "say the threshold plainly". The wrong version is therefore the one
most likely to reach print, and it makes the law look far narrower than it is
(a California-only million is a much higher bar than a global million plus
availability in the state).

The caveat's *intent* — don't assert any vendor is covered — is right and
should survive. Only the description of the threshold needs fixing.

### BLOCKING 3 — a quotation Morgan Lewis did not write

The Evidence block attributes this to Morgan Lewis, inside quotation marks:

> "applies to AI-generated images, video, and audio—but explicitly excludes
> textual content"

That sentence is not on the page. I fetched it (HTTP 200), converted it to
text and searched: **the word "explicitly" does not occur anywhere in the
document.** What Morgan Lewis actually writes is:

> By its own terms, CAITA's requirements do not apply to AI-generated textual
> content.

A fabricated quotation attributed to a named law firm is exactly the defect
class this project rejects rounds for — a claim written from what a source was
believed to say rather than from what it says. It must become a paraphrase or
the real sentence.

Related and non-blocking: **the statute contains no text-exclusion clause.**
Text is excluded by omission — the operative duties (§22757.2, §22757.3) name
"image, video, or audio content" and never text — while §22757.1(f)'s
definition of a GenAI system *does* include text. "The Act explicitly excludes
text" is therefore a shade stronger than the text supports; "the operative
duties reach image, video and audio only" is exact and just as useful.

### BLOCKING 4 — the item's title turns the statute's "or" into "AND"

Title: "the law requires a free public tool that can check image, video **AND**
audio".

Criterion (1) says "image, video, **or** audio content, or content that is any
combination thereof". Whether that obliges a particular provider to cover all
three media is a legal reading — plausible for a provider that generates all
three, but a reading nonetheless, and precisely the determination caveat 2
forbids the post from making.

The item's body quotes the disjunctive correctly. The title does not, and the
title is the first thing an author round reads and the most likely seed for
the post's thesis. Quote the "or" and let the reader see the gap; the finding
does not need the "AND".

### Non-blocking — §22757.2(a)(4)(B) is omitted

The item summarises criterion (4) as "is publicly accessible". The statute
qualifies it: "A covered provider may impose reasonable limitations on access
to the tool to prevent, or respond to, demonstrable risks to the security or
integrity of its GenAI system." Any sentence about public accessibility —
including anything about Google's waitlist — needs that clause in view.

### The strongest line available is one the item does not have (non-blocking, but take it)

OpenAI's provenance post links Sora to
`deploymentsafety.openai.com/sora-2/provenance-and-transparency-initiatives`,
which I fetched live (HTTP 200). Section 3.3, verbatim, listing OpenAI's
first-party Sora 2 provenance tooling:

> - C2PA metadata on all assets, providing verifiable origin through an
>   industry standard
> - Visible moving watermark on videos downloaded from sora.com or the Sora app
> - **Internal detection tools to help assess whether a certain video or audio
>   was created by our products.**

OpenAI says, on its own site, that its video detection tooling is **internal**.
Against a statute whose criterion (4) is "the tool is publicly accessible",
that is the cleanest sentence in this entire story and it is vendor-sourced.
The item should cite this page.

### BLOCKING 6 — "the public cannot check it" is the one claim a reader can falsify

Item body: "So OpenAI marks its video and cannot offer the public a way to
check it." CHANGELOG: "So the video is marked and **the public cannot check
it**."

The same Sora page says Sora assets carry "C2PA metadata on all assets,
providing verifiable origin through an industry standard", and the provenance
post says OpenAI became a **C2PA Conforming Generator Product** specifically so
that others can read its provenance:

> By becoming C2PA conformant, we are giving platforms a trusted way to read,
> preserve, and pass along the provenance information we attach to our content.

And Google's blog (fetched this session) says it is "adding verification for
C2PA Content Credentials ... rolling out in the Gemini app starting today, and
it will come to Search and Chrome in the coming months."

So C2PA credentials on a Sora file are readable outside OpenAI, by design and
by OpenAI's own account. "The public cannot check it" is falsifiable in one
step by anyone who knows what C2PA conformance is — and this is the sharpest,
most accusation-shaped sentence in the item, so it is the one that will be
tested hardest.

The defensible version, which loses nothing: **OpenAI offers no public way to
check its own video**, its verifier and verification API accept images and
audio only, and OpenAI says its video detection tools are internal — while the
C2PA metadata it attaches is (OpenAI's word) strippable and is not the SynthID
watermark layer its verifier exists to read.

The item needs this as a sixth caveat, in terms the author round cannot skip.

### BLOCKING 7 — "Sora ... since 2024" is not what OpenAI says

The item, the CHANGELOG and the "Done when" checklist all assert that OpenAI
says Sora output has carried Content Credentials **since 2024**. The Done-when
line is the dangerous one, because ticking it means writing that sentence.

What the post actually says:

> OpenAI has been engaged in the development and adoption of provenance
> standards **since 2024, when we began adding Content Credentials to images
> generated by DALL·E 3** ⁠ and **later** to ImageGen and Sora.

"Since 2024" attaches to DALL·E 3. Sora is "later", and undated. The safe claim
is that OpenAI says it adds Content Credentials to Sora output — which is all
the finding needs.

## Findings

### Blocking

1. **"~32 hours before the deadline" is wrong** — in the docket item and in the
   CHANGELOG headline. Real gap: 7.92 h to 2 Aug 00:00 UTC, 14.92 h to 2 Aug
   00:00 Pacific. 31.92 h is the gap to **3 August**. The "~38-hour" window
   itself is correct (38.27 h). Correcting this makes the finding sharper, not
   weaker. The CHANGELOG occurrence is the urgent one — it is permanent.
2. **"Enforcement is the Attorney General's" is wrong.** §22757.4(a)(1): "the
   Attorney General, a city attorney, or a county counsel." Confirmed in the
   codified text and in AB 853. The Evidence block's "AG-only enforcement"
   attribution to Morgan Lewis is also wrong — that firm wrote "the attorney
   general and other state actors".
3. **Caveat 1 misstates the covered-provider threshold** as
   ">1,000,000-monthly-users-in-California". §22757.1(d) puts no geographic
   qualifier on the count; the geography attaches to public accessibility. The
   item states it correctly twice elsewhere, which makes this a one-word fix in
   the one place the author is told to state it plainly.
4. **A fabricated quotation.** The Evidence block quotes Morgan Lewis as
   writing "...but explicitly excludes textual content". The word "explicitly"
   does not appear on that page. Actual sentence: "By its own terms, CAITA's
   requirements do not apply to AI-generated textual content."
5. **The item's title converts the statute's "or" into "AND"** — "a free public
   tool that can check image, video AND audio". Criterion (1) is disjunctive.
   Reading it as conjunctive is a legal determination, which caveat 2 forbids.
   The body quotes it correctly; the title is what the author round reads first.
6. **"The public cannot check it" is falsifiable as written.** OpenAI's own
   Sora 2 page says Sora carries "C2PA metadata on all assets, providing
   verifiable origin through an industry standard", and OpenAI is a C2PA
