## 1 & 2. The ordering itself, and new false positives — these converged on one real defect

Attacked the ordering rule directly with the shapes named in the brief
(multiple dots, consecutive dots, a dot-glued word that itself contains a
dot) and, separately, derived my own near-miss/decoy sweep across the full
row set. Both approaches found the **same underlying bug**, which I'm
reporting as one finding rather than two, because that's the honest
description of what happened.

**A duplicated dot inside a dotted identifier — the single most common
typo there is, a double keystroke — degrades to a real, different, wrongly
dated row, in a plain sentence with no glued word involved at all:**

```
report("simplest double-dot typo, plain sentence")
  text: "I think gpt-4..1-nano is what we use."
  hits: matchedAs="gpt-4" row.what="gpt-4-0613 (also gpt-4, ...)" shutdown=2026-10-23 replacement=gpt-5.6-sol

report("correct form, control")
  text: "gpt-4.1-nano is retiring soon"
  hits: matchedAs="gpt-4.1-nano" row.what="gpt-4.1-nano (also gpt-4.1-nano-2025-04-14)" shutdown=2026-10-23 replacement=gpt-5.6-luna
```

This is exactly the failure mode the round's own comment names as the
reason the ordering rule exists — "a confident, specific, sourced wrong
answer, strictly worse than the miss being fixed" — and it still happens.
`dotCandidatesOf()` has no floor: it keeps generating shorter and shorter
dot-truncated candidates with no bound on how far it will go, and no
check that the candidate it lands on is *related* to what was actually
typed. When a decoy or typo makes the full identifier and its one
"natural" truncation both fail to resolve, the loop keeps peeling past
them until it hits *any* real alias that happens to share a dot boundary —
even one belonging to a completely different, differently-dated row.

I confirmed this is a **regression introduced by this exact commit**, not a
pre-existing gap: before `dotCandidatesOf`'s multi-level truncation
existed, `"gpt-4..1-nano"` would have tokenized as one unmatched string and
produced a safe miss. This fix traded that safe miss for a wrong answer,
in a shape neither the "dangerous" test nor the systematic dotted-identifier
sweep tries — both only test the *clean*, single-glue case
(`"gpt-4.1-nano.Then..."` → correctly resolves to its own row on the first
try), never a case where the first and second candidates both fail before
a wrong shorter one succeeds.

**Brute-forced across all 17 dotted identifiers, the same rigor the round
applied to its own fix, rather than spot-checked:** for each, I duplicated
its first dot (`"gpt-4.1-nano"` → `"gpt-4..1-nano"`) and checked whether the
result resolves to nothing (safe), to its own row (lucky), or to a
different, wrong row (exposed):

```
4 of 17 dotted identifiers produce a WRONG-ROW collision under a duplicated-first-dot typo; 13 produce a safe miss.

EXPOSED  "gpt-4.1-nano" -> typo "gpt-4..1-nano" wrongly resolves to "gpt-4"
         (row: gpt-4-0613..., shutdown 2026-10-23, replacement gpt-5.6-sol)
         -- correct answer was shutdown 2026-10-23, replacement gpt-5.6-luna
EXPOSED  "gpt-4.1-nano-2025-04-14" -> typo "gpt-4..1-nano-2025-04-14" wrongly resolves to "gpt-4"
EXPOSED  "ft-gpt-4.1-nano-2025-04-14" -> typo "ft-gpt-4..1-nano-2025-04-14" wrongly resolves to "ft-gpt-4"
         (row: ft-gpt-4, shutdown 2026-10-23, replacement gpt-5.6-sol)
         -- correct answer was replacement gpt-5.6-luna
EXPOSED  "gpt-image-1.5" -> typo "gpt-image-1..5" wrongly resolves to "gpt-image-1"
         (row: gpt-image-1, shutdown 2026-10-23, replacement gpt-image-2)
         -- correct answer was shutdown 2026-12-01 (six weeks later), replacement happens to agree
```

All four are real, live, currently-dated rows in `RETIREMENT_DATES` — not
hypothetical decoys. Three of the four also misreport the replacement
model, not just the date. My independent decoy sweep (appending a stray
digit or letter to every one of the 77 primaries, then gluing a following
word on, checking all 77 for any resulting match) found exactly these same
three identifier families as the only ones exploitable this way:

```
scanned 77 rows' primaries, glued+off-by-one, both directions; collisions: 6
  (gpt-4.1-nano, ft-gpt-4.1-nano-2025-04-14, gpt-image-1.5 -- 2 probes each)
```

Two independent probe strategies (a plain consecutive-dot typo, and a
decoy-plus-glue sweep) converge on the identical four identifiers, which is
why I'm confident this isn't a fluke of one adversarial phrasing.

I also checked the shapes that turned out *not* to be a problem, so this
isn't overstated: a dot-glued word that itself contains a dot but resolves
cleanly on the first or second candidate (`"gpt-4.1-nano.example.com"`,
`"gpt-4.1-nano.v2.later"`, `"gpt-4.1-nano.gpt-3.5-turbo"`) all correctly
land on `gpt-4.1-nano`, never degrading further, because the correct
identifier is generated as a candidate before the truncation ever reaches
`"gpt-4"`. The bug is specifically about a *failed* intermediate candidate,
not about dots in general.

## 3. The hyphen/underscore family — second opinion: agree with the round's call

Tested the round's claim directly: hyphen-glued (`"gpt-4-0613-ish"`,
`"pre-gpt-4-0613-migration"`, `"gpt-4-turbo-2024-04-09-pinned"`),
underscore-glued (`"gpt-4-0613_legacy"`), and env-var-style
(`"GPT_4_0613"`) all correctly produce a miss, not a wrong answer, matching
the claim. I don't think any of these represents a catch a visitor would
reasonably expect and is being unfairly denied it: `-` and `_` genuinely
are structural inside the majority of this data's identifiers
(`gpt-4-turbo`, `gpt-realtime-mini`, `ft-gpt-4`...), so there's no reliable
way to tell "glued punctuation" from "part of the real name" the way a
sentence-final `.` can be told apart from a version-number `.`. The
env-var-style case (`GPT_4_0613`) is the one I'd call closest to a
reasonable ask, but fixing it means normalizing case *and* underscore/hyphen
equivalence, a different and larger feature than this round's punctuation
fix — not a punctuation-boundary problem at all. My honest second opinion:
the round's safety-over-recall call here is correct, and — pointedly — my
finding above shows the caution was well-founded: the "safer" character
(`.`) *still* produced a wrong-answer regression once the truncation logic
had no floor. Extending the same treatment to `-`, which is far more
structurally overloaded in this data than `.` ever was, would almost
certainly have been worse, not better.

## Would a stranger find this worth their attention?

This is the question three rounds of findings have deferred, and I want to
answer it on the merits rather than default to either a compliant yes or a
reflexive no just because I found something again.

The concept is good and the majority of the execution is now genuinely
careful: clean JSON, YAML, a plain sentence ending normally, a run-on
sentence with no space, vendor-prefixed pastes, curly punctuation — all of
these now resolve correctly, verified independently at every stage rather
than taken on the entry's word. That is real, substantial progress across
three rounds of adversarial pressure, and it shows in how the fixes are
built: brute-forced rather than sampled, tested in both directions, honest
about what still isn't known.

But the specific thing this tool is for — "here is your model's exact
shutdown date and the vendor's named replacement" — is precisely where I
found it can still be confidently wrong, from an ordinary typo, in data
already live today (`gpt-4.1-nano`, one of the identifiers the page's own
worked example doesn't even need to construct — it's just sitting in the
table). A visitor who mistypes one keystroke and gets told the wrong model
is dying on the wrong date with the wrong migration target has been given
something worse than no answer. That is not a rough edge for a tool whose
entire pitch is "trust this instead of scanning the table yourself" — it
is, again, the one failure mode that pitch cannot survive.

My answer: **not yet, but it is close, and it is closer for the right
reason** — because every pass has found a real, narrower defect than the
last, and the fixes have gotten more careful each time rather than more
defensive. I would forward this tool once a floor is put under
`dotCandidatesOf`'s truncation (for instance: only accept a shorter
candidate if it is a genuine token-boundary prefix of the *original*
identifier the longest candidate almost matched, not merely the first
real alias reached by blind dot-peeling) or once the truncation is bounded
to stop after the first failure rather than continuing indefinitely. That
is a real, scoped, checkable fix, not a reason to abandon the tool.
