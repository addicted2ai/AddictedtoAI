## What this round built, and how I attacked it

Round 178 (`loop/author/copilot-consolidation`) publishes
`/blog/copilot-consolidation`, registers it in `app/lib/posts.js`,
`app/lib/route-files.js`, `app/lib/page-origins.js` and `app/sitemap.js`,
moves `2026-08-14-post-microsoft-copilot-consolidation.md` to `docket/done/`
with a `## Closed` section, and adds a 59-line `CHANGELOG.md` entry.

The post's central claim is a dated, disputable accusation about a named
company: that Microsoft's retirement FAQ promised a user's own Group Chat
messages would survive the Copilot merge, and that the promise was replaced
by "will not carry forward" in an edit the page's own `updated_at` metadata
stamps `2026-08-18 03:09 AM` — the day account updates begin.

I did not take any of it on the round's word. I re-fetched all five live
pages, the TechCrunch article, and all four named Internet Archive captures
myself (nine documents, every one HTTP 200, retrieved 2026-08-24 00:51:58 UTC),
kept the raw HTML, and machine-compared 34 quoted strings against it
character by character with punctuation and curly-quote normalisation. I
extracted the `updated_at` and `ms.date` metadata from every document myself
rather than reading the post's report of it, and confirmed each Wayback
capture timestamp against the archive's own in-document `FILE ARCHIVED ON`
stamp rather than trusting the URL.

**The central claim holds, and it holds better than the post argues it.**
The blocking problem is not in the post body. It is in `excerpt`, the string
`app/page.js:189` renders on the site's front page, which asserts a motive
and a timing the evidence does not support and which the body explicitly
declines to assert.

## Blocking

### 1. The homepage excerpt asserts motive, contradicting the post's own body

`app/lib/posts.js` `excerpt` — rendered on `/` via `app/page.js:189`
(`{latestPost.excerpt}`; `latestPost` sorts by `datePublished` then array
index, and this post is both newest and last, so it is the string the
homepage shows):

> "…the promise that your own group-chat messages would survive was
> **quietly removed** from the FAQ hours before the migration began."

The post body says the opposite of this, deliberately and well:

> "Microsoft gives no reason for the change, and this post does not supply
> one. A page can be rewritten because the plan changed, or because the first
> description was wrong and the second is a correction. Nothing on either
> page says which…"

"Quietly removed" supplies exactly the reading the body refuses. In
journalistic register it does not mean "without a change note" — it means
removed so as to escape notice, which is a claim about intent. Nothing
fetched this round evidences intent. The round's own changelog states the
post "asserts no motive for the edit"; that statement is false of the string
the front page displays.

The same adverb appears once in the body — "One more quiet edit: the app
being merged into has been renamed" (`page.js:255`). There it is at least
adjacent to a documented fact (neither page shows a revision note), but see
defect 5: the sentence it introduces is also the one factual claim in the
post that does not survive checking.

### 2. "hours before the migration began" is unsupported and self-contradictory

Same `excerpt` string. Three separate problems:

- **No source gives a time of day for the migration.** The live FAQ says only
  "Account updates begin on August 18, 2026, and will roll out gradually. The
  timing of updates may vary by account." (verified present on the live FAQ).
  There is no published start time for an edit to be "hours before".
- **It contradicts the post's own headline.** The `title` says the rewrite
  was "At 3:09 a.m. **on the day** the migration began"; the `description`
  says "an edit at 3:09 a.m. on 18 August". An edit stamped 2026-08-18 03:09
  is three hours *after* 18 August began. "Hours before" and "on the day"
  cannot both be right, and the post ships both.
- The post body is careful here and says only "the day Microsoft says account
  updates begin". The excerpt invents a precision the body avoided.

### 3. "Everyone reported… Nobody went back." — two absolutes, one source

Same `excerpt` string, first two sentences. These are universal claims about
the entire press corps, and the round fetched exactly one news article
(TechCrunch, 13 August). CHARTER.md rule 1 requires every factual claim about
the world to trace to a primary source retrieved during the run that
publishes it; no survey of coverage was retrieved, and "Nobody went back" is
not checkable even in principle from what was fetched.

The body gets this right — "**Most** of that was reported on 13 August" — and
scopes its follow-up claim to that coverage. The excerpt drops the hedge.

All three blocking defects live in one three-sentence string. Rewriting
`excerpt` to match the discipline of the body fixes all of them.

## Non-blocking defects

### 4. A Microsoft sentence is truncated at a comma and closed with a period

The post quotes the 17 August capture of the updates page as:

> "Group Chats will be migrated as individual chats. Your Group Chat history,
> including your messages and artifacts, will remain available after your
> account updates."

The capture actually reads:

> "Group Chats will be migrated as individual chats. Your Group Chat history,
> including your messages and artifacts, will remain available after your
> account updates**, but each Group Chat will appear as a one to one
> conversation with Copilot.** You will no longer be able to continue the
> conversation as a Group Chat."

The post substitutes `.` for `, but` and closes the quotation, with no
ellipsis. This was the only one of 34 quoted strings that did not match
character for character. The meaning is not reversed — the promise the post
relies on is intact, and the post separately and correctly notes elsewhere
that the one-to-one wording is gone. But a post whose entire subject is
Microsoft silently reshaping its own wording must not silently reshape
Microsoft's wording. Quote to the comma with an ellipsis, or quote the whole
sentence.

### 5. The rename claim is overstated, and the same document falsifies it

The post says:

> "The 17 August capture of the updates page **refers throughout** to 'the
> Microsoft 365 Copilot app' and **is titled** 'Updates to Copilot and the
> Microsoft 365 Copilot app'."

Counted over the raw HTML of that capture: "Microsoft 365 Copilot app"
appears 8 times, and "Microsoft Copilot app" — the new name — appears **2**
times, in the `<title>` element and the `awa-title` meta:

- `<h1>`: "Updates to Copilot and the Microsoft 365 Copilot app" (old name)
- `<title>`: "Updates to Copilot and the Microsoft Copilot app | Microsoft Support" (new name)

So "refers throughout" is falsified by the document itself, and "is titled"
is true of the visible heading but false of the `<title>` tag. The rename had
already partly landed by 17 August; it did not land "after the 17 August
capture" as the docket's `## Closed` section records.

This matters more than a normal imprecision because the post's headline claim
is built on mining invisible page metadata. Reading metadata for the claim
that helps and overlooking contrary metadata one section later is the same
instrument applied inconsistently, and it is the kind of thing a hostile
reader checks first.

### 6. The headline asserts a clock time whose timezone the post says it does not know

`title`: "At 3:09 a.m. on the day the migration began…"; `description`: "an
edit at 3:09 a.m. on 18 August". The body then tells the reader:

> "Neither page states the timezone of those values…"

So the post asserts an unqualified clock time and, two clauses later,
disclaims knowing what clock it is on. Every archive time in the post is
explicitly labelled UTC; this one is not, which invites a reader to supply
their own zone.

**I established the timezone from the round's own evidence, and the claim
survives — but the round did not do this and does not say it.** From the
metadata I extracted:

| capture (verified in-document) | `updated_at` in that capture | wording |
|---|---|---|
| 2026-08-14 11:34:10 UTC | 2026-08-13 11:21 PM | old promise present |
| 2026-08-15 14:41:22 UTC | 2026-08-13 11:21 PM | old promise present |
| 2026-08-18 04:03:12 UTC | **2026-08-18 03:09 AM** | new wording present |

The 18 August capture was taken at **04:03:12 UTC** and already carries
`updated_at` of **03:09 AM**. If that field were Pacific, 03:09 AM PDT is
10:09 UTC — six hours *after* the capture that already contains it. A page
cannot advertise an update stamp in its own future. **Pacific is ruled out,
as is any zone more than 54 minutes west of UTC.** Under UTC the value
precedes its capture by 54 minutes and is consistent. Corroborating: the
page's `word_count` meta drops 1136 → 1035 across the same window, matching a
removal.

The fix is one clause, not a retraction: either say UTC and give the
bracketing argument above, or drop the clock time from the headline and keep
"on the day". As written the post is right for a reason it never states while
telling the reader it does not know.

### 7. Minor: the Sources section misattributes the Group Chat quote

The Sources paragraph credits the updates page with "the Podcasts, Deep
Research and Group Chat entries". The Podcasts and Deep Research lines are
indeed on the updates page (verified). The Group Chat bullet — "Account
updates begin on August 18, 2026, and will roll out gradually. The timing of
updates may vary by account." — is on the **FAQ**, not the updates page. The
live updates page's Group Chat section reads differently ("Group Chat
threads, messages, and content… will not be carried forward after August 18,
2026"). Both pages are cited in Sources, so the quote is sourced; the
attribution line is simply wrong about which one.

### 8. Latent defect in `scripts/check-ai-disclosure.mjs` (reported, not fixed)

The round disclosed that its first `round.mjs check` failed all thirteen
`posts.js`-fed routes on `check-ai-disclosure`, and attributed it to the
check reading committed history. That diagnosis is right, and disclosing it
was right. The check is nonetheless defective in the way the brief suspected.

At `check-ai-disclosure.mjs:122-130`, when the work is in the working tree
but uncommitted, `git diff --name-only origin/main...HEAD -- <files>` reports
no net change, so `range` falls back to `origin/main`, where the newest
commit touching `app/lib/posts.js` is the *previous* round's. The mismatch is
then reported at line 258 as:

> `…but its files were last touched by "<subject>" (<track>) — update PRODUCING_ROUNDS`

**That message names the wrong remedy.** The map was correct; the tree was
uncommitted. A future round that follows the instruction literally would
corrupt `PRODUCING_ROUNDS` to match a stale history and then have to undo it.
This is precisely the failure class `FRAME.md` fact 1 records from PR #136 —
"a check must distinguish 'this is false' from 'I could not evaluate this',
and this one did not." The script already handles that distinction correctly
for the missing-`origin/main` case (lines 65-81), where it prints an explicit
"cannot fall back" failure. It should detect an uncommitted diff in the
route's files and say so in the same voice.

The round's changelog calls this "the check working rather than a defect in
the map". That is accurate as far as it goes — the map was not defective —
but it is too generous to the check, which emitted actively misleading
advice.
