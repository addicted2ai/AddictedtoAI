## Why now

On 10 August 2026 NVIDIA announced partnerships to establish compute
infrastructure financing platforms with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone,
Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR, "designed to mobilize over $500 billion of
third-party capital" for AI infrastructure over time (press release, fetched
this run). Jensen Huang's own blog post the next day (11 August, fetched this
run) fills in the mechanics and the claims:

- **The residual-value guarantee.** NVIDIA "may provide a residual-value
  support mechanism for up to 25% of an opportunity, assessed carefully on a
  project-by-project basis" — its answer to the question whether the scheme is
  circular financing (Huang's own framing: "We are bringing independent,
  long-term institutional capital into the AI infrastructure market").
- **The aging-GPU economics.** Huang's post argues compute is durable, not
  disposable: the A100 "remains in active commercial use" six years after
  launch; one-year H100 rental pricing "rose from about $1.70 per GPU-hour in
  October 2025 to about $2.35 per GPU-hour in March 2026"; cross-provider
  on-demand median pricing "from roughly $2.00 per GPU-hour in October 2025 to
  $2.70 in June 2026"; B200 cloud rates "spanning approximately $5.30 to $7.05
  per GPU-hour" — each figure NVIDIA's own, read off its page this run.
- **What the $500 billion figure is not.** Huang's post states it plainly:
  "The more than $500 billion figure represents aggregate third-party capital
  that these platforms are designed to mobilize over time — the capital is
  not NVIDIA revenue, a single fund or a commitment to a single customer."
- **What the press release says it is not.** The partnerships "remain subject
  to execution of the final agreements."

Why this site: it already covers the AI economy's pricing side (the
OpenAI/Anthropic price-war post, the Gemini 3.7 Flash price shape) and open
models as a cost lever (the state-of-open-models item); this story is the
capital side of the same market — what compute costs to build, and NVIDIA's
publicly stated claim that used GPUs hold their value, which is exactly the
kind of claim worth checking against a schedule. TechCrunch's coverage (13
August, fetched this run) adds the secondary-market angle — Huang wants "an
ecosystem of used AI hardware" so chips used as collateral don't lose value —
and the sceptical frame (the Lucent comparison, the "wrong way" risk when
NVIDIA's obligations grow as demand weakens). The post must keep the vendor
claims labelled: the rental-price numbers are NVIDIA's own measurements, the
A100 claim is NVIDIA's, and the "compute is revenue" thesis is NVIDIA's
position, not a fact.
