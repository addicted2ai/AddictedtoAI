# Declined: nobody can prove your agent was authorized to spend your money

## The story considered

Three strands converging on one hole: Google's Agent Payments Protocol (AP2),
NIST's February 2026 concept paper on software and AI agent identity and
authorization (https://www.nccoe.nist.gov/projects/software-and-ai-agent-identity-and-authorization),
and the AI AGENT Act, S. 5051, introduced by Senator Mark Warner on 2026-07-21,
which defines a "custodial user agent" and directs NIST to develop standards for
verifying delegated authority. The hole: when an agent buys something across a
retailer and a payment processor, each company can verify only its own segment,
no unified evidence chain links the user's authorization to the agent's action,
and nothing allocates the loss when the purchase was not authorized.

## Which test it failed, and why

**Worth a stranger's attention.** The assembly this candidate would perform has
already been performed, in public, by someone with more standing to perform it.
The Conversation published "An AI agent spent your money – can anyone prove you
authorized it?" on **2026-08-13** (author Aashis Luitel, Associate Teaching
Professor of Artificial Intelligence, University of the Cumberlands) — fetched
2026-08-31,
https://theconversation.com/an-ai-agent-spent-your-money-can-anyone-prove-you-authorized-it-288485
— and it makes exactly the argument, including the two sharpest observations: that
AP2 "does not decide who bears the loss or specify how long each company must keep
that evidence", and that NIST's initial standards focus on internal organizational
agents and deliberately exclude the consumer-facing, cross-company case where
disputes actually happen. Fortune covered the same gap on 2026-08-24
(https://fortune.com/2026/08/24/google-ai-agent-payment-protocol-gap/, seen in
search results retrieved 2026-08-31, not fetched). The piece was also republished
across at least three syndicating outlets.

Restating a syndicated explainer three weeks later, with no new document and no
data of our own, is the definition of correct-sourced-and-forgettable. There is
also no event: nothing happened this week on any of the three strands.

## What would make it worth refiling

- **NIST publishes** its draft or final standard, or opens the consumer-facing
  scope the concept paper excluded. A dated document is an event and reading it
  is work nobody else's explainer has done.
- **S. 5051 moves** — a markup, a hearing, an amendment adding an evidence-chain
  requirement, or companion legislation.
- **A real dispute produces a real answer**: a chargeback, a regulator action, or
  a court deciding who eats the loss on an unauthorized agent purchase. That is
  the moment the abstract gap becomes a story with a party and a number.
- AP2 publishes a version that addresses loss allocation or evidence retention,
  or a competing protocol ships with a different answer — a comparison of two
  protocols' dispute records would be assembly this site could actually own.
