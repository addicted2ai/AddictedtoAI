# The United States tells a court that training on copyrighted text is fair use

## Why now

The government had never taken a formal position in any of these cases. It has
now taken one, in the largest of them, four days ago — and the reasoning is
written to travel: the brief addresses the Times's claims but says its analysis
applies to the book-author and publisher cases consolidated alongside. Every
subsequent filing in every one of those cases will be written against this
document. The moment to explain what it does and does not do is before the
first round of motions that cite it, not after.

It is also being reported in a way that overstates it in one direction and
understates it in another. Overstates: a statement of interest is not binding on
Judge Stein, and the government is not a party. Understates: the brief reportedly
carves training away from output and from data acquisition — which leaves
untouched the two theories that have actually produced adverse rulings against AI
companies so far, including the piracy-of-source-copies question that drove
Anthropic's $1.5B Bartz settlement. A reader who takes "DOJ says AI training is
fair use" as the whole holding will misread the next twelve months.

## Would-send test

"The Justice Department filed a brief telling a federal judge that training an
LLM on copyrighted books and articles is fair use — and argued that ruling
otherwise would help foreign adversaries." That goes to any writer, lawyer,
journalist or developer who has argued about this once, with nothing added. The
conflict-of-interest reporting is a second, independent reason to click.
