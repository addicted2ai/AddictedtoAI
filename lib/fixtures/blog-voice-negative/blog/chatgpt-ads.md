On 9 February 2026, OpenAI began testing ads in ChatGPT in the United
States. Six months and two days later, on 11 August, the same page
announced the test had launched in the United Kingdom, Mexico, Brazil,
Japan and South Korea. Add the US, Canada, Australia and New Zealand,
and a pilot that started in one country now runs in nine — with
more “this year”, per the page. The product at the centre
of it is the one OpenAI describes as used by hundreds of millions of
people, and that its own

6 August announcement

says a billion people turn to every week. Its free tier is now the
ad-funded tier.

This post reads that page —

“Testing ads in ChatGPT”

— and separates what it states from what it promises. Every date,
market, tier and control below is on the page, fetched for this post on
14 August 2026. The claims about independence and privacy are
OpenAI’s own commitments, labelled as such, because they are
exactly the claims a reader should not take on the company’s word
alone. And what the page does not say — revenue, usage impact,
any measurement that ads did not change answers — is part of the
story too.

## The arc, in the page’s own dates

The page is one post with three dated updates appended to a 9 February
original. The dates matter because they show how a cautious test became
a product:

9 February 2026 — the original post: ads
appear “for logged-in adult users on the Free and Go
subscription tiers” in the US. “Plus, Pro, Business,
Enterprise, and Education tiers will not have ads.”

26 March 2026 — OpenAI calls the early results
“encouraging” — “no impact on consumer trust
metrics, low dismissal rates of ads” — and says it will
begin expanding “starting with pilots in Canada, Australia, and
New Zealand.”

7 May 2026 — the plan goes further: expansion
to “the United Kingdom, Mexico, Brazil, Japan, and South
Korea” “in the coming weeks.”

11 August 2026 — those five markets launch:
“ChatGPT Ads has now launched in the United Kingdom, Mexico,
Brazil, Japan, and South Korea.”

That is nine markets: the US, the three announced in March, and the
five that went live on 11 August. The page never totals them —
the count is arithmetic on its own lists. The page itself is dated 11
August; its dates are the ones used here.

## Who sees ads, and who does not

The tier split has not changed since the original post: ads run
“for logged-in adult users on the Free and Go subscription
tiers,” and “Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise, and Education
tiers will not have ads.” Two exclusions are stated as part of
the test: OpenAI says it “will not show ads in accounts where the
user tells us or we predict that they are under 18,” and that ads
“are not eligible to appear near sensitive or regulated topics
like health, mental health or politics.”

For a Free-tier user who does not want ads, the page offers a trade
rather than a setting: “you can upgrade to our Plus or Pro plans,
or opt out of ads in the Free tier in exchange for fewer daily free
messages.” What “fewer” means is not quantified
anywhere on the page — and the page does not say how that sits
with the

6 August announcement

, five days earlier, that Free and Go users would get unlimited text
chats.

## The claims, as OpenAI’s claims

The page makes three commitments worth naming, because each is the kind
of claim only the company could verify, and none is verified on the
page:

Answers are independent. “Ads do not influence
the answers ChatGPT gives you. Answers are optimized based on
what’s most helpful to you.” Ads are “always clearly
labeled as sponsored and visually separated from the organic
answer.”

Advertisers never see the conversation.
“Advertisers do not have access to your chats, chat history,
memories, or personal details. Advertisers only receive aggregate
information about how their ads perform such as number of views or
clicks.”

Matching runs on the conversation, not on you. During
the test, “we decide which ad to show by matching ads submitted
by advertisers with the topic of your conversation, your past chats,
and past interactions with ads” — the page’s own
example: research recipes, “you may see ads for meal kits or
grocery delivery.”

These are OpenAI’s commitments, stated as commitments. Nothing on
the page shows its work: no audit, no measurement, no third party
named. The rest of the page’s promises are about control —
dismissing ads, sharing feedback, seeing why an ad was shown, deleting
ad data with one tap, managing ad personalization — and those,
too, are described rather than demonstrated.

## What the page does not say

Three things a reader might look for are absent. The page publishes no
ad revenue figures — nothing on what the pilot earns. It
publishes no usage data behind its March report of “no impact on
consumer trust metrics, low dismissal rates of ads”: those are
qualitative assertions with no numbers attached. And it offers no
measurement that ads did not change answers — the independence
claim is asserted, not demonstrated. “Fewer daily free
messages” is undefined too. On a page whose whole value is
checkability, the uncheckable parts are the ones worth noticing.
