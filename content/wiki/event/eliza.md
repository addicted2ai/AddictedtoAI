The first program that people mistook for a person was published as a
warning about mistaking programs for people. Joseph Weizenbaum's paper in
the January 1966 *Communications of the ACM* describes ELIZA, running on
"the MAC time-sharing system at MIT," and opens with the conversation that
made it famous: "Men are all alike. / IN WHAT WAY? / They're always bugging
us about something or other. / CAN YOU THINK OF A SPECIFIC EXAMPLE?"

The mechanism is stated in two sentences: "Input sentences are analyzed on
the basis of decomposition rules which are triggered by key words appearing
in the input text. Responses are generated by reassembly rules associated
with selected decomposition rules." There is no model of the world anywhere
in it. Weizenbaum chose the therapy setting precisely because it excuses
that absence: "the psychiatric interview is one of the few examples of
categorized dyadic natural language communication in which one of the
participating pair is free to assume the pose of knowing almost nothing of
the real world." A script — the famous one makes ELIZA "respond roughly as
would certain psychotherapists (Rogerians)," and came to be known as
DOCTOR — supplies the keywords and the pose; the program supplies nothing
else.

Weizenbaum named the program for Eliza Doolittle, and the name carries his
whole thesis: "Like the Eliza of Pygmalion fame, it can be made to appear
even more civilized, the relation of appearance to reality, however,
remaining in the domain of the playwright." His stated aim in publishing
was demystification: "once a particular program is unmasked, once its inner
workings are explained in language sufficiently plain to induce
understanding, its magic crumbles away." The paper is sixty years old and
that sentence has not needed revision.

The program itself then vanished for most of those sixty years. What
circulated — in BASIC listings, in Emacs's `M-x doctor` — were
reimplementations of reimplementations. The original was written in
MAD-SLIP for MIT's CTSS, and its source survived only as paper: "We
discovered an original ELIZA printout in Prof. Weizenbaum's archives at
MIT, including an early version of the famous DOCTOR script," reports the
team — Rupert Lane, Anthony Hay, Arthur Schwarz, David M. Berry and Jeff
Shrager — whose *ELIZA Reanimated*, posted 12 January 2025, describes "the
reanimation of this original ELIZA on a restored CTSS, itself running on an
emulated IBM 7094. The entire stack is open source." The first chatbot now
runs again on an emulation of the machine it was written for.

The strangest entry in ELIZA's record is recent. In a public online Turing
test run by Cameron Jones and Benjamin Bergen and posted 31 October 2023,
"the best-performing GPT-4 prompt passed in 49.7% of games, outperforming
ELIZA (22%) and GPT-3.5 (20%), but falling short of the baseline set by
human participants (66%)." Read that middle clause again: in 2023, a
keyword-matching script from 1966 was judged human slightly more often than
GPT-3.5, the model behind the original ChatGPT. Weizenbaum's playwright
framing holds: the relation of appearance to reality stayed in the domain
of the playwright, and the audience kept doing half the work.
