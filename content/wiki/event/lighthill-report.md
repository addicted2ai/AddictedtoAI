The Science Research Council, wanting an unbiased view of a field it was
funding, commissioned it from a famous scientist who worked in a different
one. Sir James Lighthill's "Artificial Intelligence: A General Survey" was
published early in 1973 in a Science Research Council volume titled
*Artificial Intelligence: a paper symposium*, alongside replies from Stuart
Sutherland, Roger Needham, Christopher Longuet-Higgins and Donald Michie —
the volume's own structure conceding that the survey would not go
unanswered. Its verdict on the field's record: "In no part of the field
have the discoveries made so far produced the major impact that was then
promised."

The report's structure was its argument. Lighthill divided AI into three
categories: A, advanced automation — machines doing recognizably useful
industrial, military, mathematical or scientific work; C, computer-based
research on the central nervous system; and B, deliberately labelled a
bridge — building robots — whose justification depended on the traffic it
carried between the other two. A and C, he found, deserved support on
their own merits. B, the part that was distinctively *AI*, he judged a
failure, and the mechanism he named was the "combinatorial explosion" —
the explosive growth of search that in his account stands as a general
obstacle to any self-organising system working over a large knowledge base.

John McCarthy's review says exactly what the classification cost: "his
three categories have no place for what is or should be our main scientific
activity - studying the structure of information and the structure of
problem solving processes independently of applications." And the
consequence: "missing these particular points is a consequence of missing
the existence of the AI problem as distinct from automation and study of
the central nervous system." The two were not arguing about results. They
were arguing about whether the thing named at Dartmouth in 1956 existed as
a science at all.

The argument then did something no AI dispute had done before or has done
since: it played out on national television. The BBC broadcast a debate
from the Royal Institution in June 1973 — Lighthill on one side, Donald
Michie, Richard Gregory and John McCarthy on the other. The recording runs
81 minutes and survives; Edinburgh's AI institute hosts it.

The consequences are what the report is remembered for, and the archive
that now hosts the full text — the Chilton Computing history site, a
computing-heritage project, not the funding body — states them plainly:
the report "provoked a massive loss of confidence in AI by the academic
establishment in the UK including the funding body," with effects that
persisted "for almost a decade." Recovery, by the same account, began only
with the September 1982 Research Area Review Meeting on Intelligent
Knowledge-Based Systems, whose findings fed the IKBS component of the
Alvey Programme.

Two details keep the story from flattening into martyrdom. First, the
report did not recommend abolishing the work: it supported research related
to automation and to computer simulation of neurophysiological and
psychological processes — categories A and C — and was "highly critical"
only of the foundational middle. Second, nothing in it instructed anyone to
stop funding that middle either. The collapse came from how a funding
culture read one distinguished outsider's survey. A field, it turns out,
can be defunded by taxonomy.
