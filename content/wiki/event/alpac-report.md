The report that ended the first era of machine translation is not a
critique of the science. It is an audit of a market, and that is what made
it lethal. ALPAC — the Automatic Language Processing Advisory Committee,
set up in April 1964 by the joint funding group of the Department of
Defense, the National Science Foundation and the CIA, and chaired by John
R. Pierce of Bell Telephone Laboratories — spent its first eighteen pages
not on computers but on the economics of translating Russian. Its findings:
76% of scientific articles in 1965 were already in English; a scientist
could learn to read Russian in about 200 hours; government translator
salaries were low and turnover was lower — "the facts are that the supply
exceeds demand." The Joint Publications Research Service had 4,000
translators under contract and was using about 300 of them in a given
month. Human translation cost the government between 9 and 66 dollars per
thousand words; documents sent through the Foreign Technology Division's
machine-translation pipeline took 109 days against a 65-day minimum for
human contractors.

On that ground the committee delivered the sentence the field never
outran: "There is no emergency in the field of translation. The problem is
not to meet some nonexistent need through nonexistent machine translation."
And, in the final chapter: "we do not have useful machine translation
[and] there is no immediate or predictable prospect of useful machine
translation."

The cruelest page is a comparison across a decade. The report printed
samples from four contemporary systems next to the twelve sentences of the
Georgetown-IBM demonstration of 7 January 1954 — the demo that had launched
the funding wave — and observed that "the earlier samples are more readable
than the later ones." Early machine translations of simple or selected
text, it concluded, "were as deceptively encouraging as 'machine
translations' of general scientific text have been uniformly discouraging."
John Hutchins, whose 1996 anniversary account is the best close reading of
the report, notes the methodological unfairness: general-purpose research
systems running on unprepared input were being compared with a small
demonstration built to handle a restricted set of sentences. It did not
matter.

The report's most-quoted number — that the government had spent some 20
million dollars on machine translation and closely related subjects over
the previous decade — was itself soft. Hutchins's re-audit of Appendix 16
finds the total included ALPAC's own costs, three conferences, research
abroad, and large unitemized military sums likely spent mostly on computer
equipment; spending on actual US MT research "may well have been nearer"
12 to 13 million.

What ALPAC recommended instead has aged strangely well: glossaries,
terminology databases, and machine aids for human translators —
"Machine-aided translation may be an important avenue toward better,
quicker, and cheaper translation," and "What machine-aided translation
needs most is good engineering." That is a fair description of the
translation-memory industry that arrived two decades later.

The effect on the field was not subtle. Hutchins: the report's publication
in November 1966 brought "to an end the substantial funding of MT research
in the United States for some twenty years," and "for years afterwards, an
interest in MT was something to keep quiet about; it was almost shameful."
He also records the nuance the retellings drop: the halt was neither
complete nor sudden. Wayne State's project ran until 1972, Texas until
1975, and the Georgetown project — the one whose output ALPAC printed —
had already received no funding since 1963. The word "ALPAC" itself became
the field's shorthand for a funding extinction event: at a 1984 conference
panel, researchers were still asking when the next one was due.
