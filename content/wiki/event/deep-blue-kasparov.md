There were two matches, and the second is remembered at the expense of the
first, which is a loss, because the first contains the sentence that
explains everything that followed.

Philadelphia, February 1996, over six games. Deep Blue won game one on 10
February; The Week In Chess wrote that the match "got off to the ideal
start for the sponsors with a Kasparov loss," and the Computer History
Museum marks the date for the reason everyone else did: "No computer had
ever won a game against a world champion in chess."
Kasparov then took the match 4–2, winning the final two games, and said:
"I won this match 4 to 2, but it was as tough as a world championship
match and, believe me, I played very very seriously." The sentence that
matters came after game four, when the match was level: "I'm tired from
these games and if I was playing a normal human match my opponent would
also be exhausted, but here I have something that is not exhausted and
it's playing with the same strength." The asymmetry that decided the
rematch was named by its victim fifteen months in advance.

New York, May 1997, for a purse of 1.1 million dollars. The crosstable:
Kasparov won game one; Deep Blue won game two; games three, four and five
were drawn; Deep Blue won game six. Final score 3.5 to 2.5 — the
contemporaneous TWIC report computes a performance rating of 2852 for the
machine, against Kasparov's then-rating of 2795.

The match turned on a game Kasparov did not have to lose. In game two he
resigned in a position later shown to be holdable, and he never let it go:
"I think game two posed too many questions and I think it is in the
interest of the IBM team to answer those questions," he said the following
week, having already told the closing press conference, "I resigned today.
I think the crucial game was game two... I never recovered after this
game." Game six, on 11 May, lasted 19 moves; TWIC's verdict was that
"strictly speaking game 6 was effectively resigned by Kasparov on move 7,"
his 7...h6 in a Caro-Kann being the moment the report singles out. The
champion who had ground out the 1996 comeback did not show up; the report
reads his collapse as the loss of "the mental battle with himself."

Kasparov's demand at the final press conference was not for sympathy:
"But I think it's very important for all of us to state today that Deep
Blue now must enter competitive chess." It never did. Three weeks later
TWIC was already reporting the aftermath as routine: Kasparov's "first
appearance since his debacle against Deep Blue" at a tournament in
Novgorod, other grandmasters losing exhibition games to programs — matches
the editor called "both unedifying yet inevitable" — and FIDE's president
promising to sort out the status of computers in tournaments after
consulting, of all bodies, the International Olympic Committee.

The machine's side of the story is the plainer one: an evaluation
engineered by a team, a database of grandmaster games, and search on
special-purpose hardware at a scale no human process resembles. Nothing
about the 1997 result said machines understood chess. It said that against
enough well-directed computation, that distinction stopped deciding games
— which is why this match, and not any later one, is the moment the
argument about what "real" chess understanding is went from philosophical
to practical, and why the argument has been repeating itself in other
domains ever since.
