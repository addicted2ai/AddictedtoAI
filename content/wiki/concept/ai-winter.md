"AI winter" names a mechanism, not a capability level: expectations rise past
what the work supports, disappointment follows, funders withdraw, and the
research stops for reasons that have nothing to do with whether it was
working. The folklore compresses three separate documents into one story about
overpromising. Each of the three says something narrower and more interesting
than the compression.

**ALPAC, 1966, asked whether the demand existed.** The report is titled
*Language and Machines: Computers in Translation and Linguistics*, issued by
the National Research Council. Its table of contents is the tell: chapter 10
is called "Is there a Shortage of Translators or Translation?" and runs two
pages; the chapter titled "Recommendations" is one page long. The committee's
line of attack was not that machines could not translate but that the premise
of urgent unmet demand — the thing the funding rested on — had not been
established.

**Lighthill, 1972, graded three categories and failed only the middle one.**
Sir James Lighthill's survey for the Science Research Council divided the field
into category A (advanced automation — machines built to do specific
industrial, military or scientific jobs), category C (computer-based modelling
of the central nervous system, checked against neurobiology and psychology),
and category B, the "bridge": general-purpose robotics and the claim that a
theory of intelligent machinery connects the other two. He rated progress in A
and C as respectable. Progress in B he found "even slower and more
discouraging," and the failure he named was structural: the "combinatorial
explosion," which he defined as "the explosive growth of any combinatorial
expression, representing numbers of possible ways of grouping elements of the
knowledge base according to particular rules, as the base's size increases."
The famous sentence is about promises rather than results: "In no part of the
field have the discoveries made so far produced the major impact that was then
promised." So is the one before it: "Most workers in AI research and in
related fields confess to a pronounced feeling of disappointment in what has
been achieved in the past twenty-five years." Lighthill did not need to
dispute anyone's results. He disputed the existence of the field as a field.

**The 1984 warning was published before the collapse it predicted.** The panel
that gave the phrase its currency ran at AAAI-84 in Austin and was written up
as "The Dark Ages of AI: A Panel Discussion at AAAI-84" — Drew McDermott,
M. Mitchell Waldrop, B. Chandrasekaran, John McDermott and Roger Schank — in
*AI Magazine* volume 6, number 3, Fall 1985. The published abstract records
"deep unease among AI researchers who have been around more than the last four
years or so ... that perhaps expectations about AI are too high." The industry
that unease was about had not yet turned. [Dan Weinreb's account of the
Symbolics
history](https://danluu.com/symbolics-lisp-machines/), drawing on a study of
the company, has revenues rising steadily through 1986 and then falling, with
negative earnings from 1987 to 1989. The warning was in print for roughly two
years before the numbers moved.

The practical use of the term today is as a test of specificity. Someone
forecasting a winter is forecasting one of three distinct things — that
capability stalls, that expectations correct, or that capital leaves — and the
record shows the three coming apart. Lighthill judged the work in two of his
three categories respectable and still argued the field had no coherent middle
to fund. The expectations correction was in print in 1985 while revenue was
still rising. Anyone using the word without saying which of the three links
they mean is not making a claim that can be wrong.
